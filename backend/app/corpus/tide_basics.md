# Tide Basics for UK Sea Fishing

## Understanding the Tidal Cycle

UK coastal waters experience a semidiurnal tidal pattern: two high waters and two low waters roughly every 24 hours and 50 minutes. The extra 50 minutes each day means high water occurs progressively later each day, cycling through all times of day over two weeks.

The tidal range — the height difference between high and low water — varies enormously around the UK. The Bristol Channel has the second-largest tidal range in the world, regularly exceeding 12 metres at springs. By contrast, the North Sea coast around East Anglia sees a range of only 3–4 metres.

## Spring Tides and Neap Tides

Spring tides occur twice each lunar month, around the time of the new moon and full moon. At springs, the gravitational pull of the sun and moon are aligned, producing a stronger combined force. The result is higher high waters, lower low waters, and faster tidal currents.

Neap tides occur at the quarter moons. The sun and moon are pulling at right angles, partially cancelling each other out. Neap tides have a smaller range and gentler currents.

For anglers, spring tides mean:
- Stronger currents that stir up the seabed and dislodge food items
- Greater water movement that triggers predatory behaviour in Bass and Cod
- More turbid water that gives fish confidence to feed in daylight
- Higher high water that allows access to marks only reachable at springs

Neap tides are associated with:
- Clearer, calmer water — better for clear-water species like Wrasse and Bream
- Less water movement — ideal for float fishing and light tackle
- More predictable conditions for anchoring a charter boat

## The Rule of Twelfths

Tidal flow is not constant throughout the cycle. It builds slowly from slack water, reaches maximum speed around mid-tide, then slows again. The Rule of Twelfths describes this:

- Hours 1 and 6: 1/12 of total range (slowest flow — near slack water)
- Hours 2 and 5: 2/12 of total range
- Hours 3 and 4: 3/12 of total range (fastest flow — maximum current)

Practically: fish tend to feed most actively during the middle two hours of each tide, when current is strong and oxygenated water is moving fastest over feeding grounds.

## Flood and Ebb

The flood tide is when water is rising towards high water. The ebb tide is when water is falling towards low water. Many species have a clear preference:

- Bass: classic flood-tide predator; feeds aggressively as water pushes over shallow ground and estuary mouths
- Flounder: often feeds best on the first two hours of the flood and last two of the ebb in estuaries
- Cod and Whiting: less tide-sensitive but often feed better on a running tide than at slack
- Mullet: frequently enter harbour walls and estuaries on the flood; retreat on the ebb

Slack water — the brief period around high or low water when current is minimal — produces mixed results. Some anglers find it a dead period; others target Wrasse and reef fish during slack when bait can be presented without being swept away immediately.

## Reading Tide Tables

Tide table times are given for a Standard Port (Dover, Liverpool, Avonmouth, etc.). Secondary ports use a time difference added to the standard port prediction. For example, Falmouth is approximately 6 hours before Dover HW.

Always account for British Summer Time: tide tables are published in UTC. Add one hour between late March and late October.
